Writing & saving

  • Each day has one page, and the app opens at today’s. Everything autosaves to this browser’s storage as you type; “saved” whispers in the corner when it lands.
  • There is no server and no account: entries exist only in this browser. If a save ever fails, the corner pins “not saved” until saving works again — click the notice to copy the error’s details.

Navigating

  • ⌃⌘, and ⌃⌘. step to the previous and next day that has an entry — , and . have < and > as their shifted characters. On a sub-page they step through its brother and sister sub-pages instead, in name order (numbers in reading order: 2 before 10), skipping empty ones — page through a book of sub-pages like days. A today button returns to the current day whenever the open page isn’t today’s; the journal icon in the masthead and ⌃⌘T do the same from any entry.
  • ⌃⌘B opens your bookmarks — the entries you have pinned. Press the number beside a row to go straight there, or click the row. Press A to bookmark whatever you are reading, and the × on a row to remove it. The link button on a row drops a link to that entry in at your cursor, labelled with the entry’s own name — a day by its date, a page by its name, a chapter by its heading — and says why when it can’t: no cursor in the entry, a cursor inside a code block or a link, or the entry you are already reading. The numbers are positions among the rows that have no key of their own, so removing one renumbers the rest. Each row has a key button: press it to give that row a key of its own instead of a number — type a letter b to z, then any letters or digits you like, press Enter, and typing that key takes you there from then on. A key can name what you are going to: scr12, purg19. A digit continues a key you have started and is the numbered jump only when you have not started one. A key nothing else starts with goes on the press. One that could still be the start of a longer key waits, marking the rows it could still reach, until you type the rest of it. Enter takes the marked row when only one is left, or the short key when what you typed is one in its own right; Escape backs out of a half-typed key without closing the list. Rows with a key sit together at the top, and a row that has one gives up its number, so giving a row a key makes room for one more bookmark. Nine numbered rows, and as many keyed ones as you give keys to. Bookmarks are kept on this device only.
  • ⌃⌘J opens the “Go to” row in the header — dropdowns that reach any entry. Its first dropdown picks the destination: Journal, or any page. Under Journal, pick a year and a month, and choosing a day jumps straight there — each day names its entry’s # heading; a day already holding tagged sub-entries offers them in one more dropdown, where choosing a tag jumps to it and “← the day” opens the day. Under a page, choosing a sub-page jumps to it and “← the page” opens the page; a page with no sub-pages jumps the moment you choose it, with no second dropdown. A level holding only ONE sub-page that itself has sub-pages is skipped: its children stand in its place under its name as a heading, with a row for the level itself at the top — so Virgil offers the twelve books of the Aeneid straight away, and gets its middle dropdown back the day a second work arrives. Only the last dropdown in the chain jumps — the ones before it just refill what follows. When arrowing through dropdowns by keyboard, open the list first (Space or ⌥↓) so a jump only commits on Enter. The row closes when you jump, press Escape, or press ⌃⌘J again.
  • The URL hash addresses entries directly: #2026-01-05 opens that day’s page, #2026-01-05/Ideas its “Ideas” sub-entry.
  • The date label is prefixed Today, Past, or Future to place the open entry relative to the present day.
  • When a day’s entry holds a # heading, that heading shows on its own line below the date — the same title row a sub-page uses; a day without one shows no extra line. Only a # heading counts as a day’s title (an ## is a section heading), in the header and the Go-to day list alike, and editing the heading retitles the header as you write.

Custom shortcuts

  • ⌃⌘S opens a list of your own shortcuts — short codes that stand in for longer text, handy for phrases you type often or characters that are awkward to reach. Type a code to filter the list, use ↑ and ↓ to choose, and Enter (or a click) drops that text in at the cursor.
  • Press Edit in the panel to set them up: one shortcut per line as code: expansion — the text before the first colon is the code, the rest is what it inserts. Blank lines and lines starting with # are ignored, and codes are case-sensitive (M and m are different). Shortcuts are kept on this device only.

Search

  • The header’s “Search” row scans your writing for a phrase and lists the matches with a snippet of each. Open it with ⌃⌘K or by clicking the row itself; ⌃⌘K, Escape, a click outside it, or going anywhere closes it again. Click a result — or press Enter on it — to open that entry with the match selected. Matching is case-insensitive, and straight and curly apostrophes count as the same character (don't finds don’t). Very common words show only the first 200 matches.
  • By default a word is found wherever its letters appear, so sister also finds sisters and resisters. Put an underscore at a word’s edge to require a word boundary there: _sister matches only where a word begins, sister_ only where one ends, and _sister_ the whole word on its own (so it finds sister and sister’s but not sisters). An underscore inside a word stays literal, so _created_at_ matches the whole created_at; and because the underscore marks a boundary, you can double it to search for a literal one — __private_ finds _private.
  • The row searches without leaving the entry you are in, and is there on every entry. The dropdown beside its box picks the scope: Everything, Journal — every day and its tagged sub-entries, no pages — All Pages, or any single page, which covers that page together with all its sub-pages, so a common word finds just this book’s uses. Every time you open the row it starts on the book you are reading, or on Journal in a dated entry.
  • Its results drop below the header, the full width of the window and down to the bottom of the screen. ↑ and ↓ walk the list and wrap at either end, and Enter opens the highlighted match — the top one to start with, so a search is often type-then-Enter. Inside the scope dropdown the arrows still work the dropdown.
  • Each result names where it lives: a dated entry by its date (and tag), a page by its name, and a sub-page by its title — or by its own name where it has no heading — prefixed with its book unless you have scoped the search to that one book.

Tagged sub-entries

  • “+ tagged entry” creates a named side-entry for the day, linked from the end of the main entry.
  • Selecting text and pressing Tag on the toolbar moves the selection into a new tagged entry, leaving a link in its place.
  • A day’s tagged sub-entries are listed in the masthead under the date. On a sub-entry the masthead reads as a breadcrumb — the date › the sub-entry’s name — with the date linking back to the main entry; the day’s other sub-entries stay listed below, and the masthead offers rename and delete. Sub-entries don’t nest.

Pages

  • A page is a named entry that belongs to no day — a reference document rather than a diary entry. The paper icon in the masthead lists every page in name order (numbers in reading order: 2 before 10); “New page…” at the bottom asks for a name and opens the new page. On a page the masthead reads the page’s own name — the paper icon and the Go to row are where the other pages list.
  • Pages are ordinary entries everywhere else: they autosave and back up with the rest, appear in search listed before the dated journal, and export as page/Name.md files that import back. A page’s URL is #page/its%20name, so a link to a page pastes like any entry link.
  • On a page the masthead offers rename and delete. Renaming keeps the content under the new name (links to the old name elsewhere keep pointing at the old name); deleting removes the page and returns to today. Punctuation that can’t live in a filename (\ / : * ? " < > |) becomes “-” in a name — except a title’s “: ”, which reads as “ — ” — a double dash becomes a single “-” too (“--” is what separates the parent and sub-page names in an export filename), and a page name caps at about 240 characters (a filename byte limit) — a sub-page has its own full budget, its parent’s length costing it nothing, because each name is a folder or a file of its own — so every page exports under exactly its own name.
  • A page can hold sub-pages, the way a day holds tagged entries: on a page, “+ sub-page” asks for a name, links the new sub-page from where your cursor sits (a new name asks you to place the cursor when it is elsewhere, or inside a code block or a link; an existing name just opens), and opens it — and the Tag toolbar button moves a selection into a new sub-page the same way. A name can also be a pasted article link: a dated URL (any host’s /2023/11/12/title/ shape) names the entry 2023-11-12-title, so a book of dated essays keeps chronological order — and this works in every naming prompt, renames included (a dated link whose title can’t be a name says so instead of naming the page after the URL). When a sub-page opens with a heading, the parent’s link to it takes that heading as its label (a label you wrote yourself stays). A sub-page can hold sub-pages of its own, as deep as you like — “+ sub-page” is there on every page — the same name can live under different pages, and a sub-page’s URL is #page/parent/name (#page/a/b/c deeper down). Sub-pages back up, search, and export (as page/Parent/Sub.md) like any entry — but the paper icon lists only the pages themselves: a sub-page is reached through its parent’s links, search, or a pasted link. The header’s “Go to” row lists a page’s sub-pages — pick one to jump there; on a sub-page it switches between siblings the same way. Sub-pages list in name order — except a book whose sub-page names all open with a date, which lists newest first in the Go-to row (the ⌃⌘,/. walk stays oldest-to-newest).
  • On a sub-page the masthead reads as a breadcrumb — the parent’s name › the sub-page’s name, and deeper down the whole parent path › the name — with the parent crumb linking back one level at a time. When the sub-page has a heading, that heading shows on its own line below, so a book essay imported under a number shows its title (in the header, the Go-to lists, and search results); the name stays its address. The header shows that title in full, however long, while the Go-to lists and search results shorten it to fit a row. Rename and delete work as on a tagged entry, and deleting returns to the parent page. A page whose sub-pages still hold content refuses rename and delete until they are deleted.

The bookshelf

  • The book icon in the masthead lists the bookshelf — imported books, kept beside the journal rather than in it. Each row of the list is an author, and what sits under an author is a book, which reads and edits like any other entry and holds its own parts as deep as they go — Gesta calls those parts books too, in every prompt and confirmation. Most arrive by import; “New author…” at the bottom of the list asks for a name and opens an empty one to write or paste into, the way “New page…” does, and “+ book” on an author adds a book beneath it. An author is listed and named by the heading on its own first page, not by the name it is filed under — so one stored as “Milton, John”, which is what sorts the shelf by surname, reads as “John Milton” in the bookshelf list, the go-to and search rows, the breadcrumb and a copied link. Edit that heading and every one of them follows. Pages are unchanged: a page is named by the name you gave it.
  • A book's contents page — a book below its author that holds parts of its own — folds under its ## headings: each opens closed, showing how many entries it holds, so a long contents fits on a screen. The small triangle in the margin before a heading opens and closes it; the heading's words stay editable as ever. Gesta remembers, in this browser, which sections you left open, and coming back to the page shows it as you left it, scrolled to the link you followed from it. A search result or a highlighted link that lands inside a closed section opens it. An author's page does not fold, and the text is never changed: the source view shows it whole.
  • A bookshelf entry is an ordinary entry with one difference. A link into the bookshelf that no longer exists says “no such author” or “no such book”, naming whichever is actually missing — a dead link to a renamed author says so, rather than blaming the book you were after — and leaves you exactly where you were reading, instead of quietly making a blank entry under that name — unlike a page, which is created by visiting its address.
  • Verse — a play, a poem, an extract in a journal entry or on a page — shows line numbers in a gutter down the left. What is verse is the ::: verse fence and nothing else: every line inside one is numbered, starting with 1 — or with the number you write on the fence, ::: verse 2 numbering its first line 2 — and everything outside it is prose. That is what lets a poem carry an argument above it and notes below it — neither takes a number. How often the numbers appear is yours to choose: the header’s “Line numbering” row — open it with ⌃⌘L or by clicking the row, and it appears only on a page that draws a gutter — offers line numbering options, and the gutter stays the same width whichever you pick, so the words never move. Closing the row puts your cursor back where it was. The numbers are counted fresh every time the page is drawn and are never part of the text: they are not stored, not copied with a passage, not searched, and not counted in the word count. Speaker names and stage directions are not lines, so they take no number — a line all in bold is a speaker, a line all in italic a stage direction, wherever in the verse they fall — except in a stanza, where every line is a line. While you are typing the numbers step aside — editing works on the plain text, exactly as it does anywhere else in Gesta — and they come back a moment after you stop, when the entry saves. A page with no verse block gets no gutter and no numbers at all.
  • A book of prose carries page numbers instead of line numbers. Where a page turns, ⟨15⟩ marks the spot — that is what the source view shows — and the reading view draws a small “p. 15” in the gutter beside the line the new page begins on, with a hairline at the word it begins with. Like a line number it is not part of the text: a phrase running across a page break is found by search like any other, it takes no word count, and it is not copied with a passage. Unlike a line number it is stored, because a printed page number cannot be worked out from the words — it has to be recorded. A book imported with the older <a name="#p15"> spelling is converted the first time Gesta opens after this arrives. To add page numbers to a book already imported, switch to the source view and add the number in angle brackets (e.g. ⟨15⟩) just before the first word on p. 15.
  • On an entry whose lines or sentences are numbered — with a gutter or without one — ⌃⌘G opens a small bar at the top right: type a line number, press Enter, and Gesta takes you to that line and marks it while the bar is open. Every ::: verse block counts from 1 — or from the number on its fence — so a page holding two of them may have two line 1s — Enter again with the same number goes on to the next block that has that line, and round to the first after the last, and Gesta says which block you have landed in whenever there is more than one. In a book of prose the bar asks for a page number instead — type 138, or a roman numeral like xxiv for front matter. A book with both kinds of number gets a box for each, labelled Line and Page, so there is never a prefix or a spelling to remember: the box you type in is the number you mean. Each box keeps whatever you type — until you open the bar on a different entry, where both start empty, a line or page number meaning a different place in every text — and Enter tells you what is wrong with what is there: roman and arabic mixed in one number is refused as such, and anything that is not a number for that box is named back to you. A page number names one place, so Enter again stays where it is. Escape, or the bar’s ×, closes it and leaves your cursor where you stopped: at the start of the line, or at the first word of the page. Clicking anywhere outside the bar closes it too, and leaves your cursor wherever you clicked. In a work that cites by stanza — the Faerie Queene — the Line box takes a stanza and a line instead: 2 goes to stanza 2, 2.1 to its first line, and a stanza number names one place, so there is nothing to cycle. A number the page has no line for is refused with the range of lines there are — except one below 1, which is named back instead — and a page number the book has no mark for is refused by name — page numbers neither start at 1 nor mark every turn, so there is no count to give. The marking, like the numbers themselves, is never stored, copied or searched.
  • Book pages export as bookshelf/Book/Part.md and import back the same way. Their URLs read #bookshelf/book/part, so a link to one pastes like any entry link, and search reaches them under Everything or under that book’s own row in the scope dropdown.

Markdown as you type

  • Inline, converted on the closing marker: **bold**, *italic*, ~~strikethrough~~, `code`.
  • At the start of a line, followed by a space: # heading, ## subheading, > quote, >> nested quote, - bullet, 1. numbered list. Any number works, and the list opens at it, so a countdown can start at 18. and run down. A - or a number on a fresh line inside a quote starts a list within the quote, and a # or ## starts a heading within it.
  • A list item can carry more than its own line. In the markdown view, indent a line by four spaces to hang it under the item above rather than ending the list: run straight on from the item's line for a second line of the same paragraph, or leave a blank line first to start a fresh paragraph under the item. Several may hang under one item, and consecutive indented lines stay one block, so a quoted verse keeps its line breaks. The rule is "at least as far as the item's own text", so four spaces cover every marker up to four characters wide and a countdown crossing from 14. to 9. takes the same indent throughout; a wider marker — 100., or a year read as a number — needs as many spaces as its own text is set in. Inside a quote the spaces go after the >. Once an item has a block hanging under it, Enter inside that block starts another one under the same item, and Enter again on the empty block steps out to a new item — so a run of them can be written without leaving the rendered view.
  • A line of ``` (optionally ```ruby, ```js, …) plus Enter starts a code block with syntax highlighting, a corner language label, and a copy button on hover. A code block can also sit inside a quote — in the source, keep the > on the fence lines and every code line.
  • Hovering a quote shows a copy button too, which copies the whole quote as markdown — its > markers, everything nested inside, and any pictures — ready to paste on another page. A code block, or any ::: block, inside the quote keeps its own button: whatever the mouse is innermost over wins. A grid of cards has a button of its own, copy grid, which appears when the mouse comes to just above the grid’s top-right corner or into a gap between its cards; each card inside keeps its own.
  • A copied quote or card also carries its formatting for everywhere else: paste it into an email or a document and it arrives as a quote, or as its coloured box or ruled note, pictures and all, rather than as the markdown text. Whichever form the destination understands is the one it takes.
  • -- between spaces becomes an em-dash as you type; straight quotes curl as you type — type the quote again to step it: a closing curl escapes back to a straight one, and an opening ' flips to a right single quote (the leading apostrophe in ’tis).
  • A bare URL becomes a link when finished with space or Enter, and [title](url) becomes a titled link when you type the closing paren. ⌘-click (Ctrl-click) opens a link in a new tab; a plain click on a link to an entry or page — including a full URL copied from the address bar — follows it in the same tab. A link to the entry you are already reading says so and puts your cursor back, rather than doing nothing at all.
  • Pasting several lines of text renders them as the Markdown they spell — a copied card, heading, list, table, or grid of cards arrives formatted, and two cards copied out of a grid arrive in a grid of their own. A single pasted line stays as typed, unless it is a quote — > and a space and some words — or a heading — # or ## and a space and some words — which arrives as one; a single line copied from the rendered view of an entry arrives as the Markdown it carries — an italic title in italics; anything pasted into a code block stays literal; inside a list or a table the lines arrive as plain line breaks; inside a quote they arrive as what they would mean written under the quote’s own > markers — a copied card, list, heading, or table as that block inside the quote, prose as line breaks; and the source view keeps pasted Markdown literal (it converts when you switch back).
  • Copying a selection from the rendered view puts its Markdown on the clipboard beside the formatted text, so a list, a heading, a card or an italic title pasted into another entry arrives as itself. A drag from inside one bullet, cell or verse line into the next copies as the list, table or verse block those parts came from; only a selection inside a code block copies as the browser would.

Selection toolbar

  • Selecting text floats a formatting toolbar: bold (⌘B), italic (⌘I), underline (⌘U), strikethrough, heading, quote, code block, curl quotes, Ref, Words, and Tag. Inside a code block it offers only the code-block button, which takes the block back to ordinary text; the quote and heading buttons actively damage code, and nothing else there is worth the room.
  • The curl-quotes button — or ⌘' (Ctrl-') — turns every straight quote in the selection into a curly one, handy for text pasted in from elsewhere; it also turns -- into em-dashes, closes a quote that ends speech after a dash (“stop—”), and right-singles a leading elision (’tis).
  • The Ref button — or ⌃⌘R — copies a reference to the selected passage, ready to paste into a journal entry: the source named the way a reader would write it, as a link straight back to the passage, with the passage quoted underneath. A journal entry cites as its date and a page as its title, and nothing more. In a book the line numbers of the selection go into the name; in a book of prose the page numbers do instead — pp. 22-25, or p. 22 where the passage stays on one page, read from the page it is on rather than from any marker inside it, so a passage in the middle of a page still cites that page. Where the chapter has a number of its own — Letter 12, chapter 9 — that number is the citation and the page numbers drop out; a chapter with only a title drops out itself — along with any numbered level above it, so a chapter of a numbered book leaves both behind — and lets the page numbers stand in. A printed citation gives the chapter or the page, never both. A passage with neither simply carries no numbers. A selection that crosses two verse blocks is refused — reference them one at a time — except in a work that cites by stanza, where the citation names the stanza as well as the line: I.i.2.1 for a line, I.i.2 for a whole stanza, I.i.2–3 for whole stanzas, and I.i.2.8–3.2 for a run across them.
  • ⌃⌘C copies a link to the entry you are reading — the reference’s heading line with nothing quoted under it, named the same way — ready to paste into another entry, or anywhere else as a link. It works from either view.
  • The Words button whispers the word count of the selection; ⌃⌘W does the same from the keyboard — and with nothing selected, counts the whole page. The key works in the markdown source view too, where the count skips the markdown markers.

Images

  • Paste an image to insert it; it is downscaled, recompressed, and kept in this browser, with the entry holding only a small reference to it.

Markdown source view

  • ⌃⌘M toggles the page between rendered text and its markdown source; both views are editable. Inline images appear in the source as ![](name.webp) references to the pictures filed beside the entry, and can be moved, duplicated, or deleted.
  • The toggle keeps your place. Switching there and back puts the caret where you left it. A first switch into a view, or one taken after editing in the other, matches the two texts up and carries your place across instead — close, but not always to the character, wherever the markdown and what it renders differ most.
  • A table written in the source — a header row, a | --- | --- | divider, then the body rows — renders as a table when you switch back, and imported entries carry their tables in. A table can also sit inside a quote — keep the > on each row.
  • A bullet (-) or numbered (1.) list nests: indent an item two spaces per level in the source, and the sub-list survives the switch back to rendered text. A list can also sit inside a quote — keep the > on each item line.
  • A line of prose that would otherwise read as markdown carries a leading \ in the source view — one starting -, *, 1., #, >, ``` or :::, or one indented under a list. The backslash Gesta writes marks the line as text and never shows in the rendered view; delete it and the line becomes the list, heading or quote it looks like.
  • A run of text fenced by ::: card-light-green … ::: renders as a coloured card box; the colour is the fence word — card- plus bright-yellow, brighter-yellow, light-yellow, light-green, bright-green, light-blue, bright-blue, red, pink, light-purple, or orange — and the lines inside are ordinary markdown. A card can also sit inside a quote — keep the > on the fence lines and every line inside. Hovering a card shows a copy button that copies the whole card as markdown, fences included, ready to paste on another page.
  • Cards fenced by ::: grid … ::: lay out side by side, three across, or as many across as the number after the word — ::: grid 2, ::: grid 4 — the rows falling out of the count; the grid widens past the page’s measure to fit them, no card wider than a hand’s span, and on a narrow window the count steps down. A grid holds cards and nothing else: a line of prose between them, a card without a colour, or no cards at all, and the page opens in the source view with the corner naming the line, so you can fix it there and switch back (⌃⌘M). The text inside a card edits in place; the grid’s shape — adding or removing a card, changing the count — is edited in the source view, where the fences are, and a ::: grid line typed into the rendered view stays text, as every fence does. A grid can sit inside a quote, a note, or a card, where it keeps its container’s width.
  • A run of lines fenced by ::: verse … ::: is verse, one line per line. Split a line with a | to set an original beside its translation, and write \| for a pipe you want to keep inside a line; a line with no pipe runs the full width, so a speaker name or a stage direction can stand among paired ones. A blank line inside the fence is a stanza break. Write a number after the word — ::: verse 2 — and the block’s first line is line 2, which is how lines copied out of the middle of a poem keep their numbers: a copy of lines 2 and 3 writes that fence itself, and so does the Reference button when it copies a paired block as its own fence. A fence with a number Gesta cannot read, or a ::: word it does not know, stays a paragraph and says why. An entry holding a block with translations is set wider than an ordinary one, and set to the width its longest line and translation actually need rather than to a fixed wide measure — so two entries rarely have the same width, and the prose around the block always has the width the block has. Hovering verse shows a copy button that copies the whole block as markdown, fences included; pasted somewhere that takes formatting, a paired block keeps its two columns rather than interleaving the languages. Verse can also sit inside a quote — keep the > on the fence lines and every line inside.
  • A poem in stanzas whose lines are cited within each stanza — the Faerie Queene, Book I, Canto i, stanza 2, line 1 — writes each stanza as its own fence, ::: stanza 2 … :::, the number written on the fence and required. The stanza's lines count from 1, every one of them a line, even one set all in italic, and the stanza's number stands in bold in a column left of the line numbers, whatever the line numbering is set to — and at the edge of a note, a quote or a card that holds a stanza, as the 1590 stanzas a note keeps after a canto do. How it is cited is the work's to say: its contents page carries ::: reference, roman book and canto, :::, and its entries are keyed 1.1, 1.pr for a proem, and so on — then a reference reads I.i.2.1, the book in capitals and the canto in lower case, and the masthead names the entry the same way. A passage copied from the middle of a stanza is written as a plain ::: verse fence numbered from where it starts, the citation beside it naming the stanza.
  • A run of lines fenced by ::: prose … ::: is text-and-translation prose, one sentence per line. Split a line with a | to set the original beside its translation, and write \| for a pipe you want to keep inside a sentence, in two equal columns at the wide measure; the sentences of a paragraph sit close together, and a line holding only a | — or a blank line — is a paragraph break, drawn as a wider space with a faint rule across it. A line with no pipe runs the full width. In a book, paired sentences count from 1 — or from the number on the fence, ::: prose 2 — for ⌃⌘G and for references, but the numbers are never drawn in the margin and the page keeps an ordinary left edge. Hovering the block shows a copy button that copies it as markdown, fences included, and a selected passage copied as a reference keeps both columns. Prose can also sit inside a quote — keep the > on the fence lines and every line inside.
  • A run of text fenced by ::: note … ::: is a note — a footnote, or the argument standing at the head of a book — set smaller and ringed by a thin rule. The lines inside are ordinary markdown, and a ::: verse fence can sit inside one, so a footnote can quote a poem. A note sits outside the numbering entirely: verse inside one takes no line numbers, a leaf marker inside one draws no page number and turns no page, and ⌃⌘G will not land there — write a leaf you want to show as ordinary text inside the note. An entry whose only verse is inside a note draws no line-number margin at all. A note can also go the other way round, INSIDE a ::: verse or ::: prose block: write the fence on its own line among the block's lines. The block does not end there, so the line or sentence after the note carries on counting rather than starting again at 1 — which is what a footnote in the middle of a text needs. The note takes no number of its own, runs the full width of the block with its left edge on the text's, and is left out of a passage copied as a reference. Hovering a note shows a copy button that copies the whole note as markdown, fences included; pasted somewhere that takes formatting, it arrives as its ruled, smaller box. A note can also sit inside a quote — keep the > on the fence lines and every line inside.

Enter

  • Enter starts a new paragraph; Shift+Enter breaks the line. On an empty last line, Enter steps out of a quote, code block, card, verse block, or note; in the last card of a grid it steps out of the grid too, and in any other card of a grid it adds a line, since the grid holds its cards in place — Backspace at a card’s start and Delete at its end inside a grid say so rather than joining two cards, and Enter on an empty line in the middle of a card splits it into two cards of the same colour. Enter at the end of a heading inside a quote starts a fresh line in the quote.

Tab

  • In a list, Tab nests the item under the one above it and Shift+Tab lifts it back out; the item’s own sub-list and any paragraphs hanging under it travel with it, and the items below an outdented one follow it down a level, so the order you see never changes. Select several items and they all move together — write a flat list first, then select the ones that belong under the item above them and press Tab once. An item can never sit more than one level deeper than the one above it, so Tab on the first item of a list does nothing — there is nothing above to nest under — and neither does a second Tab on an item already nested under its neighbour. Shift+Tab at the outermost level does nothing either. Each of them says so in the corner. To leave a list altogether, switch to markdown (⌃⌘M) and delete the marker. Shift+Tab straight after Tab always puts things back, the one exception being a numbered item that opened a fresh sub-list — that counts from 1, and the number it had is gone.
  • In a quote, Tab pushes the paragraph the caret is in one level deeper and Shift+Tab lifts it back out. The whole run of lines between the blank lines around it moves, so a quote of several paragraphs deepens a paragraph at a time; select across a blank line to move more than one. A heading, table, card, or grid inside a quote moves as the one thing it is, and a list inside a quote indents as a list — the innermost thing the caret is in always wins. Shift+Tab on an outermost quote does nothing and says so; to leave a quote altogether, switch to markdown (⌃⌘M) and delete the >.
  • In a code block, and in the markdown source view, Tab inserts two spaces and Shift+Tab takes up to two back off. With text selected, both work on whole lines instead: every line the selection touches shifts together, and stays selected so you can press again. ⌘A inside a code block selects just that block, so Tab can indent the whole of it; press ⌘A again for the whole page.
  • A selection that reaches outside one list, quote, or code block has nothing Tab can do to it as a whole, so Tab leaves it alone and says so — select within the one you mean.

Exporting

  • “export” writes every entry to Markdown files, in the folders that carry their keys — a day under journal/<year>/, a page under page/, a book under bookshelf/. Pasted images are saved beside each page as their own files and linked from it, so the Markdown renders anywhere. You pick the folder to fill and Gesta writes its own files into it, replacing any that have the same name. It needs a browser that can open a folder to write into (Helium or Chrome); where none is offered it writes nothing and says so, rather than leaving behind a folder that cannot be read back.
  • “import” reads an exported folder back in, and recreates all the exported entries, with their images. The main use case is to populate a new empty Gesta instance with all the entries. Import reports how many entries it will import before writing anything: if the number looks wrong, press cancel to prevent import from doing anything.

Backups

  • Point Gesta at a folder once, from “backups” → “set up automatic backups…”, and it writes the whole journal there by itself — a live copy kept current, plus dated .zip archives of whatever changed. The live copy is a true mirror: an entry you delete or rename leaves it too, so restoring from it brings back the journal as it stands rather than every state it has ever been in. The archives are the memory it deliberately lacks: one per author, per page, and per year of the journal, written at most once a day and only for what actually moved, so a book you have not opened in months costs nothing and never loses its older copies. They sit beside the live copy as archive/bookshelf/<author>/<date>.zip, archive/page/<name>/<date>.zip and archive/journal/<year>/<date>.zip. It backs up on launch and occasionally as you write, only when something changed — and leaving the tab with a backup still pending writes it right away, rather than waiting for a quiet moment that may never come. This needs a Chromium browser (Helium or Chrome) — and so does “export”, which writes into a folder you pick, so elsewhere neither is available. Opened as a file on disk, the browser asks once a session before it may write — the “backups paused — click to resume” note in the corner is that one click.
  • To roll back, import: each archive is a complete copy of one author, one page, or one year of the journal as it stood that day, so recovering an old state means unzipping the ones you want and importing them into a fresh, empty Gesta, a new browser profile or another browser, never this journal (import overwrites entry by entry and deletes nothing, so over live data it mixes old and new instead of rolling back). Which archives to unzip is what archive/manifests/<date>.txt answers: one line per author, page or year that existed that day, naming the archive holding it — so a book you deleted last month is simply absent from that day’s list rather than quietly restored alongside everything else. For one ruined entry, open its .md file inside the unzipped folder — a day sits under journal/<year>/, a page under page/ — and copy the text back.

More than one tab

  • Gesta keeps its own copy of the journal in each tab, so a tab left open since before you wrote somewhere else still holds the older text. It will not overwrite the newer version with it: a save from a tab whose copy has been overtaken is refused, and the corner says “not saved — changed in another tab, copy your text then reload”. Reload that tab and the newer text comes back. Anything typed into that tab after the notice appeared was never stored anywhere, so copy it out before you reload or move away — either of those discards it. The habit that avoids all of this is to keep one tab on whatever you are writing in; ⌘-click opens a link in a second tab, which is for reading.
  • Renaming from such a tab leaves two entries rather than one. The new name is created from the text that tab was holding, and clearing the old name is refused like any other overwrite — so the newer text stays where it was, under the name you were renaming away from. The corner says “not deleted — changed in another tab, reload”. Reload, check which of the two reads correctly, delete the other, and rename again.

The First Anniversarie: An Anatomie of the World

Wherein, by occasion of the untimely death of Mistris Elizabeth Drury, the frailtie and the decay of this whole World is represented.

When that rich soule which to her heaven is gone,
Whom all doe celebrate, who know they have one
(For who is sure he hath a soule, unlesse
It see, and Judge, and follow worthinesse,
And by Deedes praise it; Hee who doth not this,
May lodge an Inmate soule, but tis not his.)
When that Queene ended here her progresse time.
And, as t’her standing house, to heaven did clymbe,
Where loath to make the Saints attend her long,
Shee’s now a part both of the Quire, and Song.
This, world, in that great earthquake languished;
For in a common Bath of teares it bled,
Which drew the strongest vitall spirits out:
But succour’d then with a perplexed doubt,
Whether the world did loose or gaine in this,
(Because since now no other way there is,
But goodnesse, to see her, whom all would see,
All must endeavour to bee good as shee.)
This great consumption to a fever turn’d,
And so the world had fits; it joy’d, it mournd,
And, as men thinke, that Agues Physicke are,
And the Ague being spent, give over care,
So thou sicke world, mistak’st thy selfe to bee
Well, when alas, thou’rt in a Letargee.
Her death did wound and tame thee than, and than
Thou might’st have better spar’d the Sunne, or Man.
That wound was deepe, but ’tis more misery,
That thou hast lost thy sense and memory.
T’was heavy then to heare thy voice of mone,
But this is worse, that thou art speechlesse growne.
Thou hast forgot thy name, thou hadst; thou wast
Nothing but she, and her thou hast o’repast.
For as a child kept from the Fount, untill
A Prince, expected long, come to fulfill
The Cermonies, thou unnam’d hadst laid,
Had not her comming, thee her Palace made:
Her name defin’d thee, gave thee forme and frame,
And thou forget’st to celebrate thy name.
Some moneths shee hath bene dead (but being dead,
Measures of times are all determined)
But long shee’ath beene away, long, long, yet none
Offers to tell us who it is that’s gone.
But as in states doubtfull of future heyres,
When sicknesse without remedy, empayres
The present Prince, they’re loth it should be said,
The Prince doth languish, or the Prince is dead:
So mankinde feeling now a generall thaw,
A strong example gone equall to law.
The Cyment which did faithfully compact
And glue all vertues, now resolv’d, and slack’d,
Thought it was some blasphemy to say sh’ was dead;
Or that our weakness was discovered
In that confession; therefore spoke no more
Then tongues, the soule being gonne, the losse deplore.
But though it be too late to succour thee,
Sicke world, yea dead, yea putrified, since shee
Thy’ntrinsique Balme, and thy preservative,
Can never be renew’d, thou never live,
I (since no man can make thee live) will trie,
What we may gaine by thy Anatomy.
Her death hath taught us dearely, that thou art
Corrupt and mortall in thy purest part.
Let no man say, the world it selfe being dead,
Tis labour lost to have discovered.
The worlds infirmities, since there is none
Alive to study this dissection;

What life the world hath stil.

For there’s a kind of world remaining still,
Though shee which did inanimate and fill
The world, begone, yet in this last long night,
Her Ghost doth walke, that is, a glimmering light,
A faint weake love of vertue and of good
Reflects from her, on them which understood
Her worth; And though she have shut in all day,
The twi-light of her memory doth stay;
Which, from the carkasse of the old world, free
Creates a new world; and new creatures bee
Produc’d: The matter and the stuffe of this,
Her vertue, and the forme our practise is.
And thought to be thus Elemented, arme
These creatures, from hom-borne intrinsique harme,
(For all assumed unto this Dignitee,
So many weedlesse Paradises bee,
Which of themselves produce no venemous sinne,
Except some forraine Serpent bring it in)
Yet, because outward stormes the strongest breake,
And strength it selfe by confidence growes weake,
This new world may be safer, being told.

The sickenesse of the world
Impossibility of health.

The dangers and diseases of the old:
For with due temper men doe then forgoe,
Or covet things, when they their true worth know.
There is no health; Phisitians say that we
At best, enjoy, but a neutralitee.
And can there be worse sicknes, then to know
That we are never well, nor can be so?
We are borne ruinous: poore mothers cry,
That children come not right, nor orderly:
Except they headlong come and fall upon
An ominous precipitation.
How witty’s ruine? how importunate
Upon mankinde? It labour’d to frustrate
Even Gods purpose; and made woman, sent
For mans reliefe, cause of his languishment.
They were to good ends, and they are so still,
But accessory, and principall in ill.
For that first mariage was our funerall:
One woman at one blow, then kill’d us all,
And singly, one by one, they kill us now.
We doe delightfully our selves allow
To that consumption; and profusely blinde,
We kill ourselves, to propagate our kinde.
And yet we doe not that; we are not men:
There is not now that mankinde, which was then
When as the Sun, and man, did seeme to strive,

Shortnesse of life.

(Joynt tenants of the world) who should survive.
When Stag, and Raven, and the long liv’d tree,
Compar’d with man, dy’de in minoritee.
When, if a slow-pac’d starre had stolne away
From the observers marking, he might stay
Two or three hundred yeeres to see’t againe,
And then make up his observation plaine;
When, as the age was long, the sise was great:
Mans grouth confess’d, and recompenc’d the meat:
So spacious and large, that every soule
Did a faire Kingdome, and large Realme controule:
And when the very stature thus erect,
Did that soule a good way towards Heaven direct.
Where is this mankind now? who lives to age,
Fit to be made Methusalem his page?
Alas, we scarse live long enough to trie;
Whether a true made clocke run right, or lie.
Old Grandsires talke of yesterday with sorrow,
And for our children we reserve to morrow.
So short is life, that every peasant strives,
In a torne house, or field, to have three lives,
And as in lasting, so in length is man.

Smalenesse of stature.

Contracted to an inch, who was a span,
For had a man at first, in Forrests stray’d,
Or shipwrack’d in the Sea, one would have laid
A wager that an Elephant, or Whale
That met him, would not hastily assaile
A thing so equall to him: now alasse.
The Fayries, and the Pigmies well may passe
As credible; mankind decayes so soone,
We’re scarse our Fathers shadowes cast at noone.
Onely death addes t’our length: nor are we growne
In stature to be men, till we are none.
But this were light, did our lesse volumes hold
All the old Text; or had we chang’d to gold
Their silver or dispos’d into lesse glas,
Spirits of vertue, which then scattred was.
But ’tis not so: w’are not retir’d, but dampt?
And as our bodies, so our minds are crampt:
Tis shrinking, not close weaning that hath thus,
In minde and body both be-dwarfed us.
We seeme ambitious, Gods whole worke t’vndoe;
Of nothing he made us, and we strive too,
To bring our selves to nothing backe; and we
Doe what we can, to do’t so soone as he.
With new diseases on our selves we warre,
And with new Physicke, a worse Engin farre.
Thus man, this worlds Vice-Emperor, in whom
All faculties, all graces are at home;
And if in other creatures they appeare,
They’re but mans Ministers, and Legats there,
To worke on their rebellions, and reduce
Them to Civility, and to mans use.
This man, whom God did woo, and loth t’attend
Till man came up, did downe to man descend,
This man so great, that all that is, is his,
Oh what a trifle, and poore thing he is?
If man were any thing; he’s nothing now:
Helpe, or at least some time to wast, allow
T’his other wants, yet when he did depart
With her whom we lament, he lost his heart.
She, of whom th’Ancients seem’d to prophesie,
When they call’d vertues by the name of shee,
She in whom vertue was so much refin’d,
That for Allay unto so pure a minde
She tooke the weaker Sex, she that could drive
The poysonous tincture, and the stayne of Eve,
Out of her thought, and deedes, and purifie
All, by a true religious Alchemy;
See, shee is dead; shee’s dead: when thou knowest this,
Thou knowest how poore a trifling thing man is.
And learn’st thus much by our Anatomee,
The heart being perish’d, no part can be free.
And that except thou feed (not banquet) on
The supernaturall food, Religion.
Thy better growth growes whithered, and scant;
Be more than man, or thou’rt lesse then an Ant.
Then, as mankinde, so is the worlds whole frame
Quite out of joynt, almost created lame:
For, before God had made up all the rest,
Corruption entred, and deprav’d the best:
It seis’d the Angels, and then first of all
The world did in her Cradle take a fall,
And turn’d her brains, and tooke a generall maime
Wronging each joynt of th’universall frame.

Decay of Nature in other parts.

The noblest part, man, felt it first; and than
Both beasts and plants, curst in the curse of man.
So did the world from the first houre decay,
That evening was beginning of the day,
And now the Springs and Sommers which we see,
Like sonnes of women after fifty bee.
And new Philosophy cals all in doubt,
The Element of fire is quite put out;
The Sunne is lost, and th’earth, and no mans wit
Can well direct him where to looke for it.
And freely men confesse that this world’s spent,
When in the Planets, and the Firmament
They seeke so many new; they see that this
Is crumbled out againe to his Atomis.
’Tis all in pieces, all coherence gone;
All just supply, and all Relation:
Prince, Subject, Father, Sonne, are things forgot,
For every man alone thinkes he hath got
To be a Phoenix, and that then can be
None of that kinde, of which he is, but he.
This is the worlds condition now, and now
She that should all parts to reunion bow,
She that had all Magnetique force alone,
To draw, and fasten sundred parts in one;
She whom wise nature had invented then
When she observ’d that every sort of men
Did in their voyage in this worlds Sea stray,
And needed a new compasse for their way;
Shee that was best, and first originall
Of all faire copies and the generall
Steward to Fate; shee whose rich eyes, and brest:
Guilt the West-Indies, and perfum’d the East;
Whose having breath’d in this world, did bestow
Spice on those Isles, and bad them still smell so,
And that rich Indie which doth gold interre,
Is but as single money, coyn’d from her:
She to whom this world must it selfe refer,
As Suburbs, or the Microcosme of her,
Shee, shee is dead; shee’s dead: when thou knowest this,
Thou knowst how lame a cripple this world is.
And learnst thus much by our Anatomy,
That this worlds generall sicknesse doth not lie
In any humour, or one certaine part;
But as thou sawest it rotten at the heart,
Thou seest a Hectique fever hath got hold
Of the whole substance, not to be contrould.
And that thou hast but one way, not t’admit
The worlds infection, to be none of it.
For the worlds subtill immaterial parts
Feele this consuming wound, and ages darts.
For the worlds beauty is decayd, or gone,

Disformity of parts.

Beauty, that’s colour, and proportion.
We thinke the heavens enjoy their Sphericall
Their round proportion embracing all.
But yet their various and perplexed course,
Observ’d in diverse ages doth enforce
Men to find out so many Eccentrique parts,
Such divers downe-right lines, such overthwarts,
As disproportion that pure forme. It teares
The Firmament in eight and forty sheeres,
And in these constillations then arise
New starres, and old doe vanish from our eyes:
As though heav’n suffered earth quakes, peace or war,
When new Towers rise, and old demolish’t are.
They have impayld within a Zodiake
The free-borne Sun, and keepe twelve signes awake
To watch his stepps; the Goat and Crabbe controule,
And fright him backe, who els to either Pole,
(Did not these Tropiques fetter him) might runne:
For his course is not round; nor can the Sunne
Perfit a Circle, or maintaine his way
One inche direct; but where he rose to day
He comes no more, but with a cousening line,
Steales by that point, and so is Serpentine:
And seeming weary with his reeling thus,
He meanes to sleepe, being now falne nearer us.
So, of the Starres which boast that they doe runne.
In Circle still, none ends where he begunne.
All their proportion’s lame, it sinckes, it swels.
For of Meridians, and Parallels,
Man hath weaved out a net, and this net throwne
Upon the Heavens, and now they are his owne.
Loth to goe up the hill, or labour thus
To goe to heaven, we make heaven come to us.
We spur, we raigne the stars, and in their race
They’re diversly content t’obey our peace,
But keepes the earth her round proportion still?
Doth not a Tenerif, or higher Hill
Rise so high like a Rocke, that one might thinke
The floating Moone would shipwracke there, and sinke?
Seas are so deepe, that Whales being strooke to day,
Perchance too morrow, scarse at middle way
Of their wish’d jorneys ende, the bottom, die.
And men, to sound depths, so much line untie,
As one might justly thinke, that there would rise
At end thereof, one of th’Antipodies:
If under all, a Vault infernall be,
(Which sure is spacious, except that we
Invent another torment, that there must
Millions into a strait hot roome be thrust)
Then solidnesse, and roundnesse have no place.
Are these but warts, and pock-holes in the face
Of th’earth? Thinke so: But yet confesse, in this
The worlds proportion disfigured is,
That those two legges whereon it doth rely,
Reward and punishment are bent awry.
And, Oh, it can no more be questioned,
That beauties best, proportion, is dead,
Since even griefe it selfe, which now alone
Is left us, is without proportion.
Shee by whose lines proportion should bee
Examin’d measure of all Symmetree,
Whom had the Ancient seene, who thought soules made
Of Harmony, he would at next have said
That Harmony was shee, and thence infer.
That soules were but Resultances from her,
And did from her into our bodies goe,
As to our eyes, the formes from objects flow:
Shee, who if those great Doctors truely said
That the Arke to mans proportion was made,
Had beene a type for that, as that might be
A type of her in this, that contrary
Both Elements and Passions liv’d at peace
In her, who cav’d all Civill war to cease.
Shee, after whom, what forme soe’re we see,
Is discord, and rude incongruitee,
Shee, shee is dead, she’s dead; when thou knowest this,
Thou knowst how ugly a monster this world is:
And learnest thus much by our Anatomee,
That here is nothing to enamour thee:
And that, not onely faults in inward parts,
Corruptions in our brains, or in our hearts.
Poysoning the fountaines, whence our actions spring,
Endanger us: but that if every thing
Be not done fitly’nd in proportion,
To satisfie wise, and good lookers on,
(Since most men be such as most thinke they bee)
They’re lothsome too, by this Deformitee.
For good, and well, must in our actions meete;
Wicked is not much worse then indiscreet.
But beauties other second Element,
Colour, and lustre now, is as neere spent.
And had the world his just proportion,
Were it a ring still, yet the stone is gone.
As a compassionate Turcoyse which doth tell
By looking pale, the wearer is not well,
As gold fals sicke being stung with Mercury,
All the worlds parts of such complexion bee.
When nature was most busie, the first weeke,
Swadling the new borne earth God seemd to like,
That she should sport her selfe sometimes, and play,
To mingle, and vary colours every day.
And then, as though she could not make inow
Himselfe his various Rainbow did allow,
Sight is the noblest sense of any one,
Yet sight hath onely colour to feede on,
And colour is decayd: summers robe growes
Duskie, and like an oft dyed garment showes.
Our blushing redde, which us’d in cheekes to spred,
Is inward sunke and onely our soules are redde.
Perchance the world might have recovered,
If shee whom we lament had not bene dead:
But she, in whom all white, and red, and blew
(Beauties ingredients) voluntary grew,
As in an unvext Paradise; from whom
Did all things verdure, and their lustre come,
Whose composition was miraculous,
Being all colour, all Diaphanous,
(For Ayre, and Fire but thinke grosse bodies were,
And liveliest stones but drowsie, and pale to her,)
Shee, shee, is dead; she’s dead: when thou knowest this,
Thou knowest how wan a Ghost this our world is:
And learnst thus much by our Anatomee,
That it should more affright, then pleasure thee.
And that, since all faire colour then did sinke,
’Tis now but wicked vanitie to thinke,

Weakenesse in the want of correspondence of heaven & earth.

To colour vicious deeds with good pretence,
Or with bought colors to illude mens sense.
Nor in ought more this worlds decay appeares,
Then that her influence the heav’n forbeares,
Or that the Elements doe not feele this,
The father, or the mother barren is.
The clouds conceive not raine, or doe not powre
In the due birth-time, down the balmy showre.
Th’Ayre doth not motherly sit on the earth,
To hatch her seasons, and give all things birth.
Spring-times were common cradles, but are toombes,
And false conceptions fill the generall wombes.
Th’ayre showes such Meteors, as none can see,
Not onely what they meane, but what they bee.
Earth such new wormes, as would have troubled much,
Th’Egyptian Mages to have made more such.
What Artist now dares boast that he can bring
Heaven hither, or constellate any thing,
So as the influence of those starres may bee
Imprisoned in an Hearbe, or Charme, or Tree.
And doe by touch, all which those starres could doe?
The art is lost, and correspondence too.
For heaven gives little, and the earth takes lesse,
And man least knowes their trades and purposes.
If this commerce twixt heaven and earth were not
Embarr’d, and all this trafique quite forgot,
Shee, for whose losse we have lamented thus,
Would worke more fully and pow’rfully on us.
Since herbes and roots by dying, lose not all,
But they, yea Ashes too, are medicinall,
Death could not quench her vertue so, but that
It would be (if not follow’d) wondred at:
And all the world would be one dying Swan,
To sing her generall praise, and vanish than.
But as some Serpents poyson hurteth not,
Except it be from the live Serpent shot,
So doth her vertue need her here, to fit
That unto us; she working more then it.
But she, in whom, to such maturity,
Vertue was grown, past growth, that it must die,
She from whose influence all Impresion came,
But by receivers impotencies, lame,
Who, though she could not transubstantiate
All states to gold, yet guilded every state,
So that some Princes have some temperance;
Some Counsellors some purpose to advance
The common profite; and some people have
Some stay, no more then Kings should give, to crave;
Some women have some taciturnity,
Some Nunneries, some graines of chastity.
She that did thus much, & much more could doe,
But that our age was Iron, and rusty too,
Shee, shee is dead; shee’s dead: when thou knowest this,
Thou knowest how drie a Cinder this world is.
And learnst thus much by our Anatomy,
That ’tis in vaine to dew, or mollifie
It with thy Teares, or Sweat, or Blood: no thing
Is worth our travaile, griefe, or perishing,
But those rich joyes, which did possesse her heart,
Of which shee’s now partaker, and a part.

Conclusion.

But as in cutting up a man that’s dead,
The body will not last out to have read
On every part, and therefore men direct
Their speech to parts, that are of most effect;
So the worlds carcasse would not last, if I
Were punctuall in this Anatomy.
Nor smels it well to hearers, if one tell
Them their disease, who faine would thinke they’re well.
Here therefore be the end: And, blessed maid,
Of whom is meant what ever hath beene said,
Or shall be spoken well by any tongue,
Whose name refines course lines, and makes prose song,
Accept this tribute, and his first yeeres rent,
Who till his darke short tapers end be spent,
As oft as thy feast sees this widowed earth,
Will yeerely celebrate thy second birth,
That is, thy death. For though the soule of man
Be got when man is made, ’tis borne but than
When man doth die, Our bodi’s as the wombe,
And as a Mid-wife death directs it home.
And you her creatures, whom she workes upon
And have your last, and best concoction
From her example, and her vertue, if you
In reverence to her, doe thinke it due,
That no one should her prayses thus reherse,
As matter fit for Chronicle, not verse,
Vouchsafe to call to minde, that God did make
A last, and lastingst peece, a song. He spake
To Moses, to deliver unto all,
That song: because he knew they would let fall,
The Law, the Prophets, and the History,
But keepe the song still in their memory.
Such an opinion (in due measure) made
Me this great Office boldly to invade.
Nor could incomprehensiblenesse deterre
Me, from thus trying to emprison her.
Which when I saw that a strict grave could doe,
I saw not why verse might not doe so too.
Verse hath a middle nature: Heaven keepes soules,
The Grave keepes bodies, Verse the fame enroules.
