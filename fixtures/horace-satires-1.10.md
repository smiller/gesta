# Satires, 1.10

::: note
Latin: the Loeb Classical Library text (1926). Translation: H. Rushton Fairclough (Loeb, 1926).
:::

::: note
::: dual-language
[Lucili, quam sis mendosus, teste Catone | [Lucilius, how faulty you are I will prove clearly by the witness of Cato, your own advocate, who is setting to work to remove faults from your ill-wrought verses.
defensore tuo pervincam, qui male factos
emendare parat versus;
⟨…⟩hoc lenius ille, | This task is done so much more gently by him, as he is a better man, of much finer taste than the other, who as a boy was ofttimes gently entreated by the lash and moist ropes, so that later he might give aid to the poets of old against our present daintiness, when he had become the most learned of pedagogic knights.
quo melior vir est, longe subtilior illo,
qui multum puer et loris et funibus udis
exoratus, ut esset opem qui ferre poetis
antiquis posset contra fastidia nostra,
grammaticorum equitum doctissimus.
⟨…⟩ut redeam illuc:]¹ | But to return²:]
::: note
[1] *Ll.* 1-8. *These awkward verses are found in MSS. of class II only, but are not commented on by the scholiasts. Persius, an imitator of Horace, begins his third satire with* nempe. *In l.* 4, vir, *used by the writer as a long syllable, appears as* vir et *in a few later MSS.*
:::
::: note
[2] The first eight lines are regarded as spurious, and the only reason for reproducing them is that they are given in many MSS., though not in the best. The Cato referred to is Valerius Cato, a poet and critic of the late Republic, but who the *grammaticorum equitum doctissimus* was is not known. It is surely impossible “by reaching back over the relative clause intervening” to refer these words to Cato, as does Hendrickson, who upholds the genuineness of these verses.
:::

Nempe incomposito dixi pede currere versus | To be sure I did say³ that the verses of Lucilius run on with halting foot.
Lucili.
⟨…⟩quis tam Lucili fautor inepte est, | Who is a partisan of Lucilius so in-and-out of season as not to confess this?
ut non hoc fateatur?
⟨…⟩at idem, quod sale multo | And yet on the self-same page the self-same poet is praised because he rubbed the city down with much salt.
urbem defricuit, charta laudatur eadem.
::: note
[3] In *Sat.* i. 4, which may be compared with this Satire throughout.
